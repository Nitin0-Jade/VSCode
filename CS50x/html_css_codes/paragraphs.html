One

Writing code and debugging code use almost opposite mental muscles, even though they look like the same activity from the outside. Writing is generative — you're building something from nothing, holding a rough shape in your head and translating it into syntax as fast as you can before the idea slips away. Debugging is the reverse: you're not creating, you're interrogating. You already have a working system in front of you that's lying to you in some specific way, and your job is to catch it in the lie. That shift from "build" mode to "detective" mode is why context-switching between the two, even within the same five minutes, can feel more tiring than either task alone.

Two

There's also a psychological trap unique to debugging that writing code doesn't really have: the assumption that the bug is somewhere clever and hidden, when it's usually somewhere boring and obvious. A misplaced = instead of ==, a variable shadowed in the wrong scope, an off-by-one loop bound — none of these are intellectually hard once spotted, but they're maddening precisely because your brain keeps searching for a sophisticated explanation instead of rereading line four for the fifth time. Experienced programmers aren't actually smarter debuggers so much as they're faster at suspecting the boring explanation first, because they've been burned by it enough times to skip the ego trip of assuming their logic must be the problem.

Three

This is also why explaining a bug out loud to someone else — or even to a rubber duck — works so well; it's not that the other person or duck contributes anything, it's that narrating forces your brain out of "I already know how this code works" mode and into "let me actually read what's written, word by word" mode. Every programmer eventually discovers that the fastest debugging tool they own isn't a fancy IDE feature — it's the simple act of slowing down enough to stop trusting their own memory of what the code says, and actually looking at what it says.
